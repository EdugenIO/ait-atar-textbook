1. Designing usable interfaces

Recall

  • How can proximity and repetition connect related information?
  • Why does a positive opinion about a page not prove a user task works?

Visual walkthrough

Make the route and information order visible

Pause and predict: Which practical details are grouped in the revised card?

Try a small change: The same guide moves to a public touch kiosk. Which assumptions need checking?

Worked example · Make a session’s venue easier to find

  1. Initial design: The session title is prominent, but the venue is a small unlabeled icon at the bottom.

    Why this matters: Connect the element and principle to the visitor’s task.

  2. Observed task: A trial visitor finds the session time but asks where it will be held.
  3. Analysis: The practical details are separated and the icon’s meaning is unclear.
  4. Revision: Group time and venue together and add the visible label “Venue”. Preserve a clear content hierarchy.
  5. Checks: Repeat the venue task, check keyboard access to navigation and inspect the information at narrow width.
  6. Judgement: Use the results to judge the change; record the limited trial rather than claiming complete accessibility.

    Why this matters: A revision is a hypothesis until the follow-up checks provide evidence.

The analysis connects elements, principles and interaction to an observed user need.

2. Evaluating devices for a client

Recall

  • Why does more storage not compensate for insufficient RAM?
  • What should be checked before assuming an application runs on a device?

Visual walkthrough

Pass essentials before comparing extras

Pause and predict: Can C be marked passed because it has more RAM?

Try a small change: The task changes to editing the guide in a fixed office. Could B be reconsidered?

Worked example · Choose for a four-hour volunteer shift

  1. Essential requirements: A fictional schedule viewer needs App R, readable venue details and at least four hours of operation in the trial workload.

    Why this matters: Specification values become useful when related to the actual workload.

  2. Option A: A portable device supports App R and achieved five hours in the supplied trial. Venue text was readable in that trial.
  3. Option B: A fixed desktop supports App R but cannot be carried between venues without another arrangement.
  4. Option C: A portable device has more RAM, but App R support and runtime are unconfirmed.
  5. Recommendation: A is best supported by the supplied evidence for the walking task. B suits a different context; C remains unverified.
  6. Limit: The trial conditions may differ from the event. Confirm charging, environmental conditions and a fallback before deployment.

    Why this matters: Recommendations should state which essentials are passed, failed or unknown.

These are fictional observations, not measurements made for this textbook or real product claims. A justified choice states what the evidence can and cannot establish.

3. Rights, collaboration and digital change

Recall

  • Why does attribution not automatically give permission?
  • Why should a communication plan include response expectations and access needs?

Visual walkthrough

Three separate publication questions

Pause and predict: Does a credit answer all three questions?

Try a small change: A private draft becomes a public guide. What should the team revisit?

Worked example · Separate three decisions about a festival document

  1. Situation: A team receives a digital report from an Australian Government agency through an FOI process.

    Why this matters: Identify the specific right or responsibility rather than treating all rights as interchangeable.

  2. Access: The access decision concerns which document or parts can be released under that framework.
  3. Reuse: The team separately checks whether it can reproduce a photograph from the report in a public guide. Access alone does not settle this.
  4. Publication: The team checks the accuracy and implications of a proposed statement about an identifiable person.
  5. Collaboration: One person records sources and permissions; another reviews claims before an approved version is shared.
  6. Limit: Unclear legal questions are referred for appropriate advice. No real request or public post is needed for the practice task.

    Why this matters: An appropriate process does not guarantee a legal outcome; record limits and use suitable guidance.

Access, reuse and publication are related but separate checks. One approval should not be silently treated as all three.

4. Producing multimedia publications

Recall

  • How is an editable project different from an exported delivery file?
  • Why does a working link need more evidence than a screenshot?

Visual walkthrough

Coordinate picture, labels and sound over time

Pause and predict: Which separate parts would you adjust if the venue label is too brief and music masks narration?

Try a small change: The same information is needed in print. What cannot transfer unchanged?

Worked example · Coordinate a twelve-second festival introduction

  1. Content: A fictional sequence introduces the festival, shows a venue and gives the next action.

    Why this matters: Separate editable layers make targeted revisions possible.

  2. Picture tracks: Video track 1 holds the main clips. Video track 2 holds a festival title at the start and a venue label near the end.
  3. Sound tracks: Audio track 1 carries narration. Audio track 2 carries quieter supporting music where appropriate.
  4. Problem: In the supplied review scenario, the venue label disappears too quickly and music masks a spoken location.
  5. Revision: Extend the label’s useful display time and lower the music beneath the narration.
  6. Retest: Read the venue at the intended viewing size, listen to the full mix and check the text equivalent in the delivery copy.

    Why this matters: Inspect the combined delivery output, not only individual tracks.

The track structure makes separate changes possible. The result still needs a combined communication check.

5. Planning and managing a client project

Recall

  • Why is completing a task different from meeting a requirement?
  • What is a dependency, and why should an unknown stay separate from a passed check?

Visual walkthrough

Read the dependencies behind the Gantt bars

Pause and predict: Why does a ready prototype alone not establish that the build can begin?

Try a small change: Only one person is available for both day-2 tasks. What should you revisit?

Worked example · Schedule parallel work without skipping dependencies

  1. Day 1: Confirm the fictional brief and requirements.

    Why this matters: The example distinguishes calendar overlap from feasible parallel work.

  2. Day 2: Draft/review the prototype and prepare approved assets in parallel, using different available people.
  3. Days 3–4: Build only after both the prototype and required assets are ready.
  4. Day 5: Test the delivered prototype against the criteria.
  5. Day 6: Revise and retest the identified issues. This is an illustrative estimate, not a promise of fixed completion.
  6. Change case: If required assets are delayed, the dependent build may move unless a reviewed alternative or partial build is feasible.

    Why this matters: A revised plan must preserve dependencies, capacity and meaningful testing.

Parallel tasks can save calendar time only when dependencies and resource capacity allow them.
