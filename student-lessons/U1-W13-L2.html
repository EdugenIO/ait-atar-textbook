2. Understand the lesson ideas

A design plan connects what the client needs to what you will make and how you will check it. Show alternatives, explain your choice and use feedback to improve the result.

Make a small style guide

Record heading and body type treatments, colours, spacing, alignment, image treatment and writing style. Explain why these choices fit the audience and medium. Include how important information remains clear without colour alone. A style guide supports consistency across products; it should not force a layout that fails the user task.

3. Follow a worked decision

A fictional community repair café needs a campaign that helps residents find the time, venue and permitted items. The client approves content; residents use the product. Use invented records and original or approved assets.

Today’s decision in that case

A style guide records repeatable type, colour, spacing and asset decisions. Explain how each choice supports the agreed task.

  1. Identify: Which requirement, concept or distinction does this decision address?
  2. Explain: Which part of the reasoning supports the decision?
  3. Qualify: What condition or evidence would you need before claiming it works in practice?

The decision is a supplied teaching model. Its proposed checks are not your observed results.

Deeper chapter model: Make feedback change the plan
  1. Need: Residents must find the repair café venue.
  2. Criterion: A viewer can locate the venue in the phone-sized poster.
  3. Initial plan: The venue sits in a small line at the bottom.
  4. Observation: A trial viewer finds the title and date but asks where the event is held.
  5. Revision: Move the venue beside the date, increase its text size and preserve clear spacing.
  6. Retest and judgement: Ask a viewer to find the venue again. Record the result; keep testing other details and note the small trial size.

The recommendation follows observed difficulty. “I made it bigger” becomes meaningful when you explain which need it addresses and whether the change helped.

Use the chapter visual walkthrough to sketch or label the important relationships. The picture supports your explanation; it is not a replacement for checking the claim.

Supported chapter question

Check the supported reasoning

Related practice and explanation

Improve

Rewrite “we used green because it is nice” as a useful annotation.

Compare an explained answer

For example: “A dark heading on a pale background separates the title from supporting text. We will check its readability at phone size.” The actual choice needs evidence and a link to the brief.

Evaluate

A viewer likes the poster but cannot find the venue. Has the venue criterion been met?

Compare an explained answer

The positive opinion does not establish that criterion. The observed difficulty suggests a revision and retest are needed.

Transfer and test the limits

Change one relevant client condition: audience/access need, output medium, available application, recovery need or resource limit. Explain which part of today’s decision still applies, which part changes and what new evidence you need. Choose a change that matters to this lesson rather than adding an unrelated feature.

Teaching choices and review limits

Use retrieval, explicit instruction, a worked decision, faded support, self-explanation, discussion, independent application, feedback and reflection in the core route. Optional error analysis, interleaving and transfer can replace practice when appropriate. For a learner needing support, narrow the task and retain one scaffold; for a learner ready for challenge, remove the scaffold or vary a meaningful condition. No new accounts, real personal data or purchases are required.

The lesson uses selected chapter readings and a distinct scheduled-task model. Native application operation, approved online collection, representative access trials and independent subject review are not established by the supplied text. Formal assessment follows the school’s approved conditions.
