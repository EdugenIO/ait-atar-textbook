# Unit 1 learning workbook

Classroom draft. Use alongside student chapters 1.1–1.8, covering teaching weeks 1–15 (four 55-minute lessons each week). These activities are embedded in class time. Write in your notebook or a copy of this file. Fictional client: a community repair café.

## 1. Understanding a design brief

### Recall

- Think of a poster or website you used recently. What did you need to find?
- What made that information easy or difficult to locate?

### Guided practice

The client asks for “a professional-looking poster”. Complete: audience → information needed → design criterion → evidence.

### Explain

1. Identify: A school librarian commissions a guide for new students. Who is the client, and who is the audience?
2. Improve: Rewrite “the page should be easy” as a testable criterion.
3. Analyse: Why might the same layout fail when moved from a printed noticeboard to a phone image?

### Apply: Write the repair café brief

1. List the client, audience, purpose and intended medium.
2. Record four pieces of required content and two constraints.
3. Write three criteria that could be checked using evidence.
4. Compare two existing or sketched layouts against those criteria.
5. Identify one question you still need to ask the client.

Evidence to keep: A one-page brief, three criteria, a comparison and one unresolved client question.

Check your work:

- The purpose describes an audience action or understanding.
- Criteria describe observable results.
- The comparison uses evidence rather than preference.

Exit question: Write one design criterion and describe the evidence you would use to evaluate it.

## 2. Using the elements of design

### Recall

- Name the audience and purpose of the repair café poster.
- Recall a criterion you could use to judge its communication.

### Guided practice

A poster uses a detailed photograph behind a small date label. Identify two elements you could change and predict the effect.

### Explain

1. Distinguish: A circle and a shaded sphere appear in a graphic. Which primarily shows shape and which shows form?
2. Explain: Why is “I used blue” an incomplete annotation?
3. Apply: How could you show two categories without relying on colour alone?

### Apply: Create an element study

1. Make seven small labelled visual studies, one for each element.
2. Choose a poster detail and identify at least three elements in it.
3. Create two alternatives by changing one element at a time.
4. Annotate the intended effect and relate it to the brief.
5. Compare the alternatives at their actual viewing size.

Evidence to keep: Seven labelled studies, two controlled alternatives and annotations explaining their effects.

Check your work:

- All seven elements are correctly identified.
- The alternatives isolate a change so its effect can be compared.
- Annotations connect an observation to an audience need.

Exit question: Describe one element in your current design and propose a change you could test.

## 3. Organising a design with principles

### Recall

- Name three design elements without looking back.
- Explain the difference between observing a visual choice and judging its effect.

### Guided practice

A poster has three unrelated sections with different heading fonts and equal gaps between every item. Suggest changes that could improve unity.

### Explain

1. Explain: Can an asymmetrical design be balanced?
2. Analyse: Why might six equally dominant headings make a poster harder to use?
3. Distinguish: How do repetition and proximity support unity in different ways?

### Apply: Redesign the information order

1. Sketch two versions of the repair café poster using the same content.
2. Create a different dominant component in each version.
3. Use proximity and repetition to group related details.
4. Draw the intended viewing path and annotate the elements supporting it.
5. Ask a partner what they noticed first; compare the observation with your intention.
6. Revise one decision and justify it using the brief and feedback.

Evidence to keep: Two compositions, annotated viewing paths, partner observations and one justified revision.

Check your work:

- Annotations connect elements to principles.
- The hierarchy supports the brief.
- Feedback informs a specific revision.

Exit question: Write a three-part annotation: visual choice → design principle → audience need.

## 4. Typography, grids and composition

### Recall

- Explain how size and tone can establish emphasis.
- Recall how proximity can connect a heading with its supporting information.

### Guided practice

A mobile poster uses centred paragraphs, tight line spacing and a very small venue label. Choose two changes and explain how you would check them.

### Explain

1. Distinguish: How is typeface different from size?
2. Apply: How could a grid help when the date, image and heading appear disconnected?
3. Evaluate: A partner finds the date quickly but misses the booking instruction. What should your evaluation include?

### Apply: Finish and evaluate a composition

1. Create two layouts using the same brief, with one based on a grid and one exploring the rule of thirds.
2. Specify typeface, size, alignment, formatting and spacing decisions.
3. Select the better-supported layout and add five detailed annotations.
4. Ask a viewer to find the date, venue and next action at the intended output size.
5. Record observations, revise one issue and compare the result with the brief.

Evidence to keep: Two layouts, five annotations, a task-based feedback record and a revised design.

Check your work:

- Typography choices are described precisely.
- Compositional rules are applied and evaluated.
- The final judgement uses evidence against criteria.

Exit question: Choose one annotation from your design. Improve it by adding a precise effect and a way to test it.

## 5. Choosing hardware and software

### Recall

- What is the difference between a client preference and a requirement?
- Name one way to check whether a digital product meets its brief.

### Guided practice

An editor requires 8 GB RAM and 10 GB free storage. A supported computer has 4 GB RAM and 200 GB free storage. Does the large amount of free storage fix the problem? Explain.

### Explain

1. Trace: A student records speech, applies a noise filter, listens through headphones and saves a file. Identify input, processing, output and storage.
2. Distinguish: What could happen to an unsaved edit and a previously saved project if the computer loses power?
3. Explain: Why could an older application fail on a newer computer?
4. Justify: A team needs to record interviews away from school. Give one reason to choose a mobile system and two checks before using it.

### Apply: Recommend a complete recording setup

1. Use the repair café announcement brief from the worked example. List the input, processing, output and storage needed.
2. Draw a labelled system diagram that distinguishes CPU, RAM and saved storage.
3. Compare options A and B against each stated requirement. Mark unknown details as unknown.
4. Recommend a microphone and listening device by function; explain the connection and support checks required.
5. Explain how the operating system supports the editor and controls access to the saved project.
6. Write a short recommendation with one limitation and a practical test. Use supplied specifications; you do not need to buy or install anything.

Evidence to keep: A system diagram, compatibility table and justified recommendation with a test plan.

Check your work:

- RAM and secondary storage have different roles in the diagram.
- The recommendation checks every stated requirement rather than choosing the largest numbers.
- The test uses a sample of the intended work and states a limitation.

Exit question: Explain why a computer with more RAM could still be the wrong choice for a particular application.

## 6. Media files and spreadsheet tools

### Recall

- Why does a file that saves successfully still need testing on the target system?
- What is the difference between RAM and saved storage?

### Guided practice

A student uses =VLOOKUP("A02",$A$2:$C$4,2,FALSE) with the worked table. Predict the result and explain why it is not 8.

### Explain

1. Choose: A logo must appear at several sizes. Why might SVG be useful, and what must you still check?
2. Trace: What does =HLOOKUP("V03",$B$1:$D$2,2,FALSE) return for the horizontal example?
3. Debug: A sort moves only the quantity column. Why is this dangerous?
4. Explain: Why should an unknown resource code not automatically receive quantity zero?

### Apply: Prepare a small campaign asset register

1. Create the vertical reference table from the worked example and the horizontal table from section 7.
2. Enter P01, A02, V03 and X99 as lookup tests. Predict results before entering the two formulas.
3. Record the observed result and explain the missing-code case.
4. Add a delivery-format column and justify a choice for a logo, photograph and spoken audio.
5. Sort complete rows by quantity, then filter one format. Check that records still match their original codes.
6. Record folder names, editable masters, delivery copies and one software licence check.

Evidence to keep: A spreadsheet or hand-traced table, predicted and observed results, format decisions and a short file plan.

Check your work:

- Both lookup directions use exact matching.
- Missing codes are flagged without invented quantities.
- Sorting preserves records and format choices match the intended output.

Exit question: Explain how you would distinguish a wrong return-column number from a missing lookup code.

## 7. Creating and communicating responsibly

### Recall

- What source information should travel with a downloaded asset?
- Why is a free download not proof of unrestricted reuse?

### Guided practice

A classmate used a photograph in private study notes. Can the group assume it is also cleared for the public campaign page?

### Explain

1. Distinguish: What is the difference between permission and attribution?
2. Apply: What should a source log say if a licence permits reuse but requires credit and an indication of changes?
3. Respond: A project chat shares a humiliating edited picture of a student. What should a bystander do?
4. Evaluate: Why might a moderated forum suit project discussion better than a fast group chat?

### Apply: Check a fictional campaign before sharing

1. Create a source log for three supplied or original mock assets. Do not publish them.
2. Mark each intended use as supported by documented terms, awaiting clarification or requiring replacement.
3. Write a model credit and a note of any edits for one asset.
4. Compare a forum, group chat and virtual world for the team’s communication needs.
5. Write a response to a fictional harmful-post scenario without reproducing harmful material.
6. Agree response windows, moderation expectations and a way to ask for help.

Evidence to keep: A source log, publication decisions, a model credit and a team communication plan.

Check your work:

- Permission and attribution are checked separately.
- Unclear rights lead to clarification or replacement.
- The communication plan reduces harm and respects time boundaries.

Exit question: Explain one reason a fully referenced asset might still need replacing before publication.

## 8. From brief to design plan

### Recall

- How can you turn “looks good” into a criterion you can check?
- Which design choices can help a viewer locate the date and venue?

### Guided practice

A team wants a static poster and a short three-scene promotional video. Which planning representations would help each product, and why?

### Explain

1. Distinguish: How does a thumbnail differ from a storyboard?
2. Improve: Rewrite “we used green because it is nice” as a useful annotation.
3. Evaluate: A viewer likes the poster but cannot find the venue. Has the venue criterion been met?
4. Reflect: Why keep a record of a change that did not improve the design?

### Apply: Create and review a campaign design plan

1. Write a one-paragraph situation analysis and confirm the audience, purpose and medium.
2. Create a criteria table with at least three needs and a check for each.
3. Sketch two thumbnail layouts; select one using those same criteria.
4. Create a short style guide and five annotations linking choices to audience needs.
5. Build a prototype using supplied or original assets and record a viewer’s task observations.
6. Revise one issue, retest and evaluate each criterion with evidence and one limitation.

Evidence to keep: Brief, criteria table, two alternatives, style guide, annotated prototype and feedback/revision record.

Check your work:

- Every main choice can be traced to the brief.
- The planning representation suits the product.
- The final judgement separates observations, intentions and limitations.

Exit question: Describe one design decision, the criterion it supports and the observation you would collect to check it.

## Explained answers: attempt the tasks first

### Chapter 1

Guided reasoning: One possible response: local residents → date and venue → include clearly labelled date and venue beneath the title → ask a resident to locate both and record any difficulty. “Professional” needs clarification with the client; this criterion addresses a specific communication need.

1. The librarian is the client; new students are the audience. They may have different priorities, so explain how the guide serves the students while meeting the client’s requirements.

2. For example: a new student can find the library opening hours from the first page. Explain how you would test this, such as observing a student attempting that task.

3. The viewing size and interaction context change. Text that was readable at print size may become hard to read when scaled down. Check the actual output size rather than assuming the original arrangement will work.

Independent comparison: A plausible brief targets local residents, communicates attendance information and uses a phone-viewed poster. Criteria might check the presence and findability of date, venue and accepted items. The actual event details and distribution requirements need client confirmation.

### Chapter 2

Guided reasoning: Reduce the visual texture behind the date by using a simpler background; increase the tonal difference between text and background. Both may help separate the date from surrounding detail. Check the revised output at its intended size.

1. The flat circle shows shape. The sphere suggests three-dimensional form through visual depth cues such as shading.

2. It names a colour without explaining how it works in the design or why it helps the intended audience. Add the effect and its connection to a requirement.

3. Use clear text labels and a consistent difference in shape, pattern or placement. The appropriate combination depends on the product and user task.

Independent comparison: For example, keep the date wording and position fixed while increasing tonal difference in one alternative and space around the label in another. Explain what changed and how each version could be checked. There is no single required colour or layout.

### Chapter 3

Guided reasoning: Repeat a consistent heading treatment for related sections and adjust proximity so each heading is grouped with its own information. Explain which parts belong together. Do not assume every font must be identical if a meaningful hierarchy needs distinction.

1. Yes. Visual weight depends on factors such as size, tone, position and complexity. Different components can counterbalance one another without being mirror images.

2. They compete for attention and may hide the intended priority. Adjust their relationships so the main message is recognised before supporting information.

3. Repetition connects components through recurring visual treatment. Proximity connects them by grouping related items spatially. Both can help the viewer recognise relationships.

Independent comparison: A defensible version makes the event name dominant, groups date and venue together and directs attention toward the next action. Another order may be justified for a different brief. Use observed behaviour to evaluate the choice rather than treating one layout as universally correct.

### Chapter 4

Guided reasoning: For example, use a consistent left alignment for multi-line practical information and increase the separation or prominence of the venue label. Inspect the actual output and ask a viewer to find the venue. Record whether the changes address the observed difficulty.

1. Typeface describes the letter design family; size describes how large the letters are displayed or printed. Both influence the appearance and readability of text.

2. Choose common alignment lines and consistent margins, then position related components against them. Check that the resulting groupings express the information relationships.

3. Identify the successful date treatment and the unsuccessful instruction, connect both observations to criteria, propose a specific revision and describe how you would retest it.

Independent comparison: There is no single prescribed layout. A strong response shows deliberate alternatives, consistent alignment, readable practical information and a revision supported by feedback. Explain why the selected arrangement serves this brief better than the alternative.

1. Its vector paths scale well. Check that the target software supports the file and that any embedded raster images have enough resolution.

### Chapter 8

Guided reasoning: Use thumbnail alternatives for the poster’s layout. Use a storyboard for the video’s three scenes, noting visuals, narration and order. A wireframe would be useful if the product also had an interface or navigation to plan.

1. A thumbnail quickly explores a layout idea. A storyboard shows a sequence across panels, such as scenes over time.

2. For example: “A dark heading on a pale background separates the title from supporting text. We will check its readability at phone size.” The actual choice needs evidence and a link to the brief.

3. The positive opinion does not establish that criterion. The observed difficulty suggests a revision and retest are needed.

4. It shows what was tried and prevents treating an unsuccessful idea as proven. Use the result to choose a different revision.

Independent comparison: A strong plan links the event details to clear groups, uses consistent type treatments and records a meaningful comparison of alternatives. Its review reports observed tasks, explains a specific revision and states what the limited trial cannot establish. Different layouts can be valid when justified with evidence.
