1. Understanding a design brief

Recall

  • Think of a poster or website you used recently. What did you need to find?
  • What made that information easy or difficult to locate?

Visual walkthrough

Turn a request into a decision

Pause and predict: The client says “make it exciting”. Which step is still missing before you choose a layout?

Try a small change: The poster will now be viewed on a phone instead of a noticeboard. Which decision and check would you revisit?

Try it with support

The client asks for “a professional-looking poster”. Complete: audience → information needed → design criterion → evidence.

Practise and explain

Identify: A school librarian commissions a guide for new students. Who is the client, and who is the audience?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Improve: Rewrite “the page should be easy” as a testable criterion.

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Analyse: Why might the same layout fail when moved from a printed noticeboard to a phone image?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Write the repair café brief

  1. List the client, audience, purpose and intended medium.
  2. Record four pieces of required content and two constraints.
  3. Write three criteria that could be checked using evidence.
  4. Compare two existing or sketched layouts against those criteria.
  5. Identify one question you still need to ask the client.

Evidence to keep: A one-page brief, three criteria, a comparison and one unresolved client question.

A fresh client: the school library

The library needs a phone-friendly guide for new Year 11 students. It must show opening hours, where to borrow a laptop and how to ask for help. The librarian has not supplied the final hours.

Name the client and audience. Write one testable criterion, one design decision and one question for the client.

Exit question

Write one design criterion and describe the evidence you would use to evaluate it.

2. Using the elements of design

Recall

  • Name the audience and purpose of the repair café poster.
  • Recall a criterion you could use to judge its communication.

Visual walkthrough

Seven elements you can see

Pause and predict: A dark rectangle sits behind a date. Which two elements create the panel and separate it from a pale background?

Try a small change: Keep the wording, size and position fixed. Add more space around the date. What changed, and how could you check its effect?

Try it with support

A poster uses a detailed photograph behind a small date label. Identify two elements you could change and predict the effect.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: A circle and a shaded sphere appear in a graphic. Which primarily shows shape and which shows form?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Explain: Why is “I used blue” an incomplete annotation?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Apply: How could you show two categories without relying on colour alone?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Create an element study

  1. Make seven small labelled visual studies, one for each element.
  2. Choose a poster detail and identify at least three elements in it.
  3. Create two alternatives by changing one element at a time.
  4. Annotate the intended effect and relate it to the brief.
  5. Compare the alternatives at their actual viewing size.

Evidence to keep: Seven labelled studies, two controlled alternatives and annotations explaining their effects.

A fresh context: a sports-club notice

A sports club needs a notice saying “Training moved to Court 2”. The background photograph makes the small text hard to read.

Change two elements. Explain what each change could do, and how you would check the result.

Exit question

Describe one element in your current design and propose a change you could test.

3. Organising a design with principles

Recall

  • Name three design elements without looking back.
  • Explain the difference between observing a visual choice and judging its effect.

Visual walkthrough

Compare relationships between the same items

Pause and predict: In the planned hierarchy, which details form a group? What makes their relationship visible?

Try a small change: Make the action line larger than the title. What might happen to the viewing path?

Try it with support

A poster has three unrelated sections with different heading fonts and equal gaps between every item. Suggest changes that could improve unity.

Practise and explain

Explain: Can an asymmetrical design be balanced?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Analyse: Why might six equally dominant headings make a poster harder to use?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Distinguish: How do repetition and proximity support unity in different ways?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Redesign the information order

  1. Sketch two versions of the repair café poster using the same content.
  2. Create a different dominant component in each version.
  3. Use proximity and repetition to group related details.
  4. Draw the intended viewing path and annotate the elements supporting it.
  5. Ask a partner what they noticed first; compare the observation with your intention.
  6. Revise one decision and justify it using the brief and feedback.

Evidence to keep: Two compositions, annotated viewing paths, partner observations and one justified revision.

A fresh context: a festival guide

A festival page gives its title, sponsor list and “Find a stage” control equal prominence. Visitors mainly need to locate their next event.

Choose a dominant item, group related information and describe the viewing path you would test.

Exit question

Write a three-part annotation: visual choice → design principle → audience need.

4. Typography, grids and composition

Recall

  • Explain how size and tone can establish emphasis.
  • Recall how proximity can connect a heading with its supporting information.

Visual walkthrough

Use a grid to organise; use thirds to explore

Pause and predict: The date and venue almost line up, but their left edges differ slightly. What would a shared grid line help you change?

Try a small change: Your design looks clear on a large editing screen but the venue is hard to read on a phone. What should you revise and retest?

Try it with support

A mobile poster uses centred paragraphs, tight line spacing and a very small venue label. Choose two changes and explain how you would check them.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: How is typeface different from size?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Apply: How could a grid help when the date, image and heading appear disconnected?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Evaluate: A partner finds the date quickly but misses the booking instruction. What should your evaluation include?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Finish and evaluate a composition

  1. Create two layouts using the same brief, with one based on a grid and one exploring the rule of thirds.
  2. Specify typeface, size, alignment, formatting and spacing decisions.
  3. Select the better-supported layout and add five detailed annotations.
  4. Ask a viewer to find the date, venue and next action at the intended output size.
  5. Record observations, revise one issue and compare the result with the brief.

Evidence to keep: Two layouts, five annotations, a task-based feedback record and a revised design.

A fresh context: a canteen menu

A phone menu uses the same text size for food names, prices and section headings. Prices almost line up.

Sketch a clearer hierarchy and alignment. Explain how you would test it with a student.

Exit question

Choose one annotation from your design. Improve it by adding a precise effect and a way to test it.

5. Choosing hardware and software

Recall

  • What is the difference between a client preference and a requirement?
  • Name one way to check whether a digital product meets its brief.

Visual walkthrough

From microphone to saved recording

Pause and predict: You have edited the recording but have not saved the change. Which copy is at risk if power fails?

Try a small change: The SSD has plenty of free space, but the computer has less RAM than the editor requires. Has the RAM requirement been met?

Try it with support

An editor requires 8 GB RAM and 10 GB free storage. A supported computer has 4 GB RAM and 200 GB free storage. Does the large amount of free storage fix the problem? Explain.

Practise and explain

Trace: A student records speech, applies a noise filter, listens through headphones and saves a file. Identify input, processing, output and storage.

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Distinguish: What could happen to an unsaved edit and a previously saved project if the computer loses power?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Explain: Why could an older application fail on a newer computer?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Justify: A team needs to record interviews away from school. Give one reason to choose a mobile system and two checks before using it.

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Recommend a complete recording setup

  1. Use the repair café announcement brief from the worked example. List the input, processing, output and storage needed.
  2. Draw a labelled system diagram that distinguishes CPU, RAM and saved storage.
  3. Compare options A and B against each stated requirement. Mark unknown details as unknown.
  4. Recommend a microphone and listening device by function; explain the connection and support checks required.
  5. Explain how the operating system supports the editor and controls access to the saved project.
  6. Write a short recommendation with one limitation and a practical test. Use supplied specifications; you do not need to buy or install anything.

Evidence to keep: A system diagram, compatibility table and justified recommendation with a test plan.

A fresh context: a field interview

The school radio team will record a ten-minute interview outdoors. Its desktop editor is not available on the team’s phone.

Recommend a workflow rather than just a device. Identify two checks and one limitation.

Exit question

Explain why a computer with more RAM could still be the wrong choice for a particular application.

6. Media files and spreadsheet tools

Recall

  • Why does a file that saves successfully still need testing on the target system?
  • What is the difference between RAM and saved storage?

Visual walkthrough

Follow an exact lookup to its result

Pause and predict: Change the return-column number from 3 to 2. What is returned?

Try a small change: Keep exact matching but search for X99. What should you investigate?

Try it with support

A student uses =VLOOKUP("A02",$A$2:$C$4,2,FALSE) with the worked table. Predict the result and explain why it is not 8.

Practise and explain

Choose: A logo must appear at several sizes. Why might SVG be useful, and what must you still check?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Trace: What does =HLOOKUP("V03",$B$1:$D$2,2,FALSE) return for the horizontal example?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Debug: A sort moves only the quantity column. Why is this dangerous?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Explain: Why should an unknown resource code not automatically receive quantity zero?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Prepare a small campaign asset register

  1. Create the vertical reference table from the worked example and the horizontal table from section 7.
  2. Enter P01, A02, V03 and X99 as lookup tests. Predict results before entering the two formulas.
  3. Record the observed result and explain the missing-code case.
  4. Add a delivery-format column and justify a choice for a logo, photograph and spoken audio.
  5. Sort complete rows by quantity, then filter one format. Check that records still match their original codes.
  6. Record folder names, editable masters, delivery copies and one software licence check.

Evidence to keep: A spreadsheet or hand-traced table, predicted and observed results, format decisions and a short file plan.

A fresh context: a library resource list

A library table uses book codes, titles and shelf numbers. A missing book code gives #N/A.

Explain how to investigate the error and sort the list safely.

Exit question

Explain how you would distinguish a wrong return-column number from a missing lookup code.

7. Creating and communicating responsibly

Recall

  • What source information should travel with a downloaded asset?
  • Why is a free download not proof of unrestricted reuse?

Visual walkthrough

Check the use before the credit

Pause and predict: You know the creator’s name but cannot find reuse terms. Is the asset ready for a public post?

Try a small change: The planned use changes from private notes to a public website. Which check must you revisit?

Try it with support

A classmate used a photograph in private study notes. Can the group assume it is also cleared for the public campaign page?

Practise and explain

Distinguish: What is the difference between permission and attribution?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Apply: What should a source log say if a licence permits reuse but requires credit and an indication of changes?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Respond: A project chat shares a humiliating edited picture of a student. What should a bystander do?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Evaluate: Why might a moderated forum suit project discussion better than a fast group chat?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Check a fictional campaign before sharing

  1. Create a source log for three supplied or original mock assets. Do not publish them.
  2. Mark each intended use as supported by documented terms, awaiting clarification or requiring replacement.
  3. Write a model credit and a note of any edits for one asset.
  4. Compare a forum, group chat and virtual world for the team’s communication needs.
  5. Write a response to a fictional harmful-post scenario without reproducing harmful material.
  6. Agree response windows, moderation expectations and a way to ask for help.

Evidence to keep: A source log, publication decisions, a model credit and a team communication plan.

A fresh context: a sporting-club page

A team wants to publish a member’s photograph and add background music found online.

List the separate checks before posting, including the people shown.

Exit question

Explain one reason a fully referenced asset might still need replacing before publication.

8. From brief to design plan

Recall

  • How can you turn “looks good” into a criterion you can check?
  • Which design choices can help a viewer locate the date and venue?

Visual walkthrough

Represent the idea, then test the task

Pause and predict: Which representation best shows the order of scenes in a short video?

Try a small change: The product becomes an interactive guide. What extra plan would help?

Try it with support

A team wants a static poster and a short three-scene promotional video. Which planning representations would help each product, and why?

Practise and explain

Distinguish: How does a thumbnail differ from a storyboard?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Improve: Rewrite “we used green because it is nice” as a useful annotation.

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Evaluate: A viewer likes the poster but cannot find the venue. Has the venue criterion been met?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Reflect: Why keep a record of a change that did not improve the design?

Write or sketch your response: __________________________________

Create and review a campaign design plan

  1. Write a one-paragraph situation analysis and confirm the audience, purpose and medium.
  2. Create a criteria table with at least three needs and a check for each.
  3. Sketch two thumbnail layouts; select one using those same criteria.
  4. Create a short style guide and five annotations linking choices to audience needs.
  5. Build a prototype using supplied or original assets and record a viewer’s task observations.
  6. Revise one issue, retest and evaluate each criterion with evidence and one limitation.

Evidence to keep: Brief, criteria table, two alternatives, style guide, annotated prototype and feedback/revision record.

A fresh context: an open-day guide

Visitors need to find a session room on a phone. A prototype has attractive photographs but the room label is hard to locate.

Choose a planning representation, a revision and a test.

Exit question

Describe one design decision, the criterion it supports and the observation you would collect to check it.
