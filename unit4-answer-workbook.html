1. Protecting data and planning recovery

Worked practical companions: Unit 4 requirement-linked worked evidence · Source verifiability, accuracy and currency case

Recall

  • What is the difference between a claim and evidence that supports it?
  • Why should a plan include checks as well as production tasks?

Pause and predict: I1 is missing. Which illustrated route still has its required backup set?

Show the explanation

Try a small change: The failure occurs after new Wednesday edits but before the next backup. What is not protected by Tuesday’s recovery point?

Compare your reasoning

Worked example · Recover a Wednesday booking list

  1. Sunday: Make full backup F of the selected booking data.

    Why this matters: Read each backup’s reference point before selecting a restore sequence.

  2. Monday: Record changes A and complete backup I1 in the incremental scheme, or D1 in the differential scheme.
  3. Tuesday: Record new changes B and complete I2, or D2. D2 includes changes since Sunday.
  4. Wednesday failure: The computer fails before another backup. The latest available recovery point is Tuesday’s completed backup.
  5. Incremental restore: Use F → I1 → I2 in order. This simple chain needs both increments.
  6. Differential restore: Use F → D2. D1 is not required for this recovery point.
  7. Check: Open sample bookings, check expected records and report any work after the recovery point as potentially lost.

    Why this matters: A complete-looking schedule still needs usable copies and a successful recovery check.

These are alternative backup schemes, not instructions to mix both chains. The example assumes successful, usable backups and a supported restore process.

Try it with support

In the example, I1 is lost but F and I2 remain. Can you assume a complete Tuesday restore?

Example reasoning: No. The simple incremental chain requires I1 as well as I2. Check other recovery copies rather than claiming the chain is complete.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: “Cloud storage automatically means we have a tested backup.” Is that justified?

No. Check retention, deletion behaviour, protected recovery copies and a successful restore. Remote location alone does not establish recoverability.

Apply: In the example, I1 is lost but F and I2 remain. Can you assume a complete Tuesday restore?

No. The simple incremental chain requires I1 as well as I2. Check other recovery copies rather than claiming the chain is complete.

Compare: Does a data mart have to be stored on the office computer?

No. Its defining feature is its focused analysis purpose, not its physical location. It can be hosted in the cloud.

Explain: Will encrypting the only copy recover an accidentally deleted file?

No. Encryption addresses confidentiality. Recovery needs a suitable backup or other tested recovery mechanism.

Evaluate: A chart links warm weather to higher attendance. Does it prove heat causes attendance?

No. Check other factors, data quality and the study design before making a causal claim.

Example response: A sound proposal prioritises bookings, sets recovery needs and identifies complete backup sets. It protects recovery copies and assigns roles. A sample restore provides limited evidence, while a written plan alone does not prove recovery will succeed.

Visual walkthrough

Follow a value through the validation checks

Compare your reasoning

Update instructions and both validation layers. Test 30 as accepted and 31 as rejected; 21 now passes the range rule. Keep required, lower-boundary and whole-number checks.

Worked example · Test a fictional group-size field

  1. Rule: The field is required and must contain a whole number from 1 to 20 inclusive.

    Why this matters: Explicit rules determine expected results before testing.

  2. Empty: Reject: explain that a group size is required.
  3. 0 or 21: Reject: explain the allowed range.
  4. 1 and 20: Accept under this field’s rules: both boundaries are included.
  5. 2.5 or letters: Reject: request a whole number in the allowed range.
  6. Server check: A real collection service must repeat the rules on the server. A browser message alone cannot secure submitted data.
  7. Limit: An accepted number does not prove that this many people will actually attend.

    Why this matters: Keep validation behaviour separate from truthfulness, identity and permission.

Test valid, invalid and boundary cases. Explain what each test establishes and what it leaves unknown.

3. Designing and justifying a secure LAN

Worked practical companions: Unit 4 requirement-linked worked evidence

Recall

  • How do a switch, router and access point have different roles?
  • Why does encryption not replace access control or recovery backups?

Visual walkthrough

Follow a request through roles and boundaries

Pause and predict: Does the visitor boundary shown here prove that isolation works?

Show the explanation

No. The equipment must enforce suitable separation and access rules, and tests must check allowed and blocked paths.

Try a small change: The upstream internet link fails. Must every local task stop?

Compare your reasoning

Not necessarily. Local printing or file access may continue if their local equipment and required services remain available. Identify each actual dependency.

Reason from observations

What does this fault rule out?

The staff printer and local files work. One external booking service fails on one laptop.

  1. Separate observations from explanations.
  2. Give two possible causes still consistent with the observations.
  3. Choose a comparison that would narrow the possibilities.
Compare your reasoning after trying

Local success is evidence that some local functions work, not proof that every network component is healthy. Compare another device and another external service; consider device settings, name resolution, connectivity and service-specific faults.

Worked example · Propose a LAN for the community centre

  1. Requirements: Two office computers and one printer need fixed connections; a staff laptop needs mobility. Visitors must not access staff files.

    Why this matters: Link the component choice to a specific task, location or boundary.

  2. Local layout: Use a central switch for the fixed devices. Connect an appropriate access point for the staff laptop. Confirm ports, compatibility and installation constraints.
  3. Visitor boundary: Use supported network separation and access rules for visitor internet access. Label the boundary; do not assume another network name alone provides isolation.
  4. External route: Connect through the router and suitable upstream service equipment. Use HTTPS for the booking-service interaction.
  5. Protection: Specify firewall restrictions, unique staff credentials and controlled physical access to equipment. Plan file recovery separately.
  6. Evidence: Check staff tasks, visitor isolation, required room coverage and external access. Record failed and untested items separately.
  7. Limit: No site measurements or configuration tests are supplied. This is a justified draft proposal, not a verified installation.

    Why this matters: The final statement separates a proposed design from measured or tested evidence.

Each component and control has a reason tied to a requirement. The visitor boundary needs explicit configuration and testing, not merely a label on a picture.

Try it with support

The staff printer works, but the booking website fails on one laptop. Does this prove that the router has failed?

Example reasoning: No. Local printing confirms some local communication, not the whole route. Compare authorised devices and services, then inspect relevant connection, application and upstream evidence. Do not replace a device or disable controls on the strength of one symptom.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: Which jobs belong to TCP and IP?

TCP supplies a reliable ordered byte stream between applications when the connection succeeds. IP addresses and forwards packets without itself guaranteeing delivery or order.

Compare: Why might one design use Ethernet for desks and wireless for a roaming laptop?

The fixed devices do not need mobility; wireless supports movement. Compare installation, coverage, interference, capacity and required equipment for the actual site.

Trace: Which simplified path can a staff laptop use to reach the external booking service?

Laptop → access point → switch → router → upstream network → service, with return traffic following permitted routes. Actual devices may combine roles.

Evaluate: Is a visitor Wi-Fi name enough evidence that staff files are isolated?

No. The underlying separation and access rules must be supported, configured and tested.

Explain: Why do passwords, firewalls and a locked equipment area complement each other?

They address different risks: authentication/access, traffic control and physical access. None replaces the others or proves complete security.

Diagnose: Staff files are accessible but only one external service fails. What can you conclude?

Some local services work. The failure is not yet localised; compare the affected service and relevant paths before attributing a cause.

  3. Annotate the roles of TCP/IP and HTTPS in the assumed web-service journey.
  4. Compare Ethernet and wireless for two different client tasks.
  5. Add firewall, credential and physical-security choices, linking each to a risk.
  6. Write expected-result tests for staff tasks, visitor isolation, coverage and an upstream outage.
  7. Use the supplied troubleshooting scenario to write evidence-based next checks and revise one unsupported claim.

Evidence to keep: Annotated LAN diagram, protocol explanation, connection comparison, security reasoning and a test/troubleshooting log.

Example response: A defensible proposal links fixed devices through a switch, supports staff mobility with a checked access-point arrangement and uses enforced visitor separation. It justifies each control, explains the assumed HTTP/2/TLS/TCP/IP journey and records untested coverage and configuration claims.

Worked example · Teach a volunteer to check an activity request

  1. Objective: Select a listed workshop and check a whole-number group size from 1 to 12 using fictional values.

    Why this matters: The model states why the input meets a rule, rather than asking the learner to copy it blindly.

  2. Model: Choose Design workshop and enter 4. Explain that 4 is a whole number within the stated range.
  3. Guided trial: Keep the workshop selected and try 13. Predict the message, check it and explain the upper boundary.
  4. Correction: Replace 13 with 12. Explain why the upper boundary is included and check again.
  5. Independent trial: Choose Media workshop, predict what happens with 2.5 and then correct it to a valid whole number.
  6. Feedback: Explain the violated rule and give a clear next action without deleting unrelated entries.
  7. Limit: A valid practice value meets the stated rules; it does not reserve a place, prove attendance or verify server-side handling.

    Why this matters: The final limit separates successful validation from real-world truth and untested service behaviour.

The sequence moves from an explained model to supported correction and independent use of the same rules.

Practise and explain

Plan: What is more useful than “learn how forms work” as an objective for this tutorial?

For example: select a listed workshop and enter a whole-number group size from 1 to 12, using the instructions to correct an invalid value. This can be observed.

Compare: When could an asynchronous tutorial be useful, and what support might it still need?

It can be revisited at a convenient time and pace. Learners may still need clear examples, alternatives and an appropriate way to ask questions.

Apply: Why leave real names and email addresses out of this practice form?

They are not needed for the learning task. Fictional workshop and size values let students explore behaviour without collecting personal information.

Test: What should happen for 12, 13 and 2.5 under the stated rule?

Accept 12 as an included boundary. Reject 13 as above the range and 2.5 as not a whole number. Also test empty and lower-boundary cases.

Evaluate: A form tool shows no keyboard focus. Can correct validation compensate?

No. Keyboard interaction needs its own attention. Validation behaviour and accessible operation are related but distinct checks.

Distinguish: Does the offline demo establish that an online form stores responses safely?

No. The demo does not submit or store responses. A real service needs its actual data flow, permissions, retention and receiving-side behaviour checked.

Build a small tutorial and fictional-data form

  1. Define an audience, prerequisite and observable task objective.
  2. Choose an approved available tool or the local demonstration as a starting point; justify relevant functions.
  3. Write a concise explanation and complete model, then a guided and independent practice task.
  4. Provide suitable media alternatives and check the instructions with keyboard, enlarged text and narrow layout.
  5. Specify only the fictional fields needed for practice, with labels, rules and clear error messages.
  6. Run the planned valid, invalid and boundary cases and record expected versus observed results.
  7. Observe a trial learner, revise an issue and retest. If using an online tool, inspect sharing and response-handling settings without collecting personal data.

Evidence to keep: Tutorial plan and resource, field specification, test log and an observation/revision record.

Example response: A suitable tutorial models Design workshop with 4, guides correction of 13 to 12 and asks the learner to reason about 2.5 independently. Its form needs no personal details, has explicit labels and errors, and is checked with planned cases and learner observations.

Worked example · Choose a service and evaluate the centre guide

Practise and explain

Calculate: In the simplified 30-day model, what unavailable time corresponds to a 99.9% target?

30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes; 0.1% is 43.2 minutes. This arithmetic does not replace the agreement’s measurement definitions or predict an outage.

Distinguish: Why should service type be checked before comparing targets?

Targets may cover different components or scopes. Compare like-for-like service commitments and the complete user task.

Evaluate: A client approves the colours but a keyboard task fails. Is the product fully accepted against that task criterion?

No. Positive visual feedback does not repair the interaction failure. Revise and retest the failed criterion.

Improve: What is wrong with “How excellent was our easy-to-use guide?” as a survey question?

It leads the respondent and assumes the desired conclusion. Ask a neutral question about a defined task, with meaningful response options.

Compare: Why might a small satisfaction survey and a usability observation disagree?

They measure different things and may involve different people or contexts. Inspect the task evidence, questions and sample rather than treating one number as universal proof.

Reflect: What makes self-reflection more useful than “I worked hard and the project was good”?

Link a decision to evidence, identify a limitation, explain a revision and state what you would change next time.
