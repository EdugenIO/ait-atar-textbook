1. Understanding a design brief

Recall

  • Think of a poster or website you used recently. What did you need to find?
  • What made that information easy or difficult to locate?

Visual walkthrough

Turn a request into a decision

Pause and predict: The client says “make it exciting”. Which step is still missing before you choose a layout?

Show the explanation

Find out what the audience needs to know or do. “Exciting” does not tell you which information matters or how to check success.

Try a small change: The poster will now be viewed on a phone instead of a noticeboard. Which decision and check would you revisit?

Compare your reasoning

Check the text size and arrangement at the actual phone viewing size. Ask someone to locate the date and venue again; the original layout may no longer be readable.

Try it with support

The client asks for “a professional-looking poster”. Complete: audience → information needed → design criterion → evidence.

Example reasoning: One possible response: local residents → date and venue → include clearly labelled date and venue beneath the title → ask a resident to locate both and record any difficulty. “Professional” needs clarification with the client; this criterion addresses a specific communication need.

Practise and explain

Identify: A school librarian commissions a guide for new students. Who is the client, and who is the audience?

The librarian is the client; new students are the audience. They may have different priorities, so explain how the guide serves the students while meeting the client’s requirements.

Improve: Rewrite “the page should be easy” as a testable criterion.

For example: a new student can find the library opening hours from the first page. Explain how you would test this, such as observing a student attempting that task.

Analyse: Why might the same layout fail when moved from a printed noticeboard to a phone image?

The viewing size and interaction context change. Text that was readable at print size may become hard to read when scaled down. Check the actual output size rather than assuming the original arrangement will work.

Write the repair café brief

  1. List the client, audience, purpose and intended medium.
  2. Record four pieces of required content and two constraints.
  3. Write three criteria that could be checked using evidence.
  4. Compare two existing or sketched layouts against those criteria.
  5. Identify one question you still need to ask the client.

Evidence to keep: A one-page brief, three criteria, a comparison and one unresolved client question.

Example response: A plausible brief targets local residents, communicates attendance information and uses a phone-viewed poster. Criteria might check the presence and findability of date, venue and accepted items. The actual event details and distribution requirements need client confirmation.

A fresh client: the school library

The library needs a phone-friendly guide for new Year 11 students. It must show opening hours, where to borrow a laptop and how to ask for help. The librarian has not supplied the final hours.

Name the client and audience. Write one testable criterion, one design decision and one question for the client.

Compare with an example response

The librarian is the client; new Year 11 students are the audience. A criterion could be that a student can locate laptop-borrowing instructions on the first screen. Group those instructions under a clear heading. Confirm the opening hours rather than inventing them.

Exit question

Write one design criterion and describe the evidence you would use to evaluate it.

2. Using the elements of design

Recall

  • Name the audience and purpose of the repair café poster.
  • Recall a criterion you could use to judge its communication.

Visual walkthrough

Seven elements you can see

Pause and predict: A dark rectangle sits behind a date. Which two elements create the panel and separate it from a pale background?

Show the explanation

Shape creates the rectangle. Tone creates the difference in lightness between the dark panel and pale background.

Try a small change: Keep the wording, size and position fixed. Add more space around the date. What changed, and how could you check its effect?

Compare your reasoning

Space changed. Compare both versions at the same viewing size and ask someone to find the date. Record whether the extra space helps distinguish it from nearby information.

Try it with support

A poster uses a detailed photograph behind a small date label. Identify two elements you could change and predict the effect.

Example reasoning: Reduce the visual texture behind the date by using a simpler background; increase the tonal difference between text and background. Both may help separate the date from surrounding detail. Check the revised output at its intended size.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: A circle and a shaded sphere appear in a graphic. Which primarily shows shape and which shows form?

The flat circle shows shape. The sphere suggests three-dimensional form through visual depth cues such as shading.

Explain: Why is “I used blue” an incomplete annotation?

It names a colour without explaining how it works in the design or why it helps the intended audience. Add the effect and its connection to a requirement.

Apply: How could you show two categories without relying on colour alone?

Use clear text labels and a consistent difference in shape, pattern or placement. The appropriate combination depends on the product and user task.

Create an element study

  1. Make seven small labelled visual studies, one for each element.
  2. Choose a poster detail and identify at least three elements in it.
  3. Create two alternatives by changing one element at a time.
  4. Annotate the intended effect and relate it to the brief.
  5. Compare the alternatives at their actual viewing size.

Evidence to keep: Seven labelled studies, two controlled alternatives and annotations explaining their effects.

Example response: For example, keep the date wording and position fixed while increasing tonal difference in one alternative and space around the label in another. Explain what changed and how each version could be checked. There is no single required colour or layout.

A fresh context: a sports-club notice

A sports club needs a notice saying “Training moved to Court 2”. The background photograph makes the small text hard to read.

Change two elements. Explain what each change could do, and how you would check the result.

Compare with an example response

For example, place the message in a plain rectangular panel (shape) and use a dark message on a pale panel (tone). Check the actual viewing size and ask someone to read the new location. A different solution can work if supported by evidence.

Exit question

Describe one element in your current design and propose a change you could test.

3. Organising a design with principles

Recall

  • Name three design elements without looking back.
  • Explain the difference between observing a visual choice and judging its effect.

Visual walkthrough

Compare relationships between the same items

Pause and predict: In the planned hierarchy, which details form a group? What makes their relationship visible?

Show the explanation

The date and venue are close together in one group. Their proximity connects the practical details, while separation from the title distinguishes their role.

Try a small change: Make the action line larger than the title. What might happen to the viewing path?

Compare your reasoning

The action may attract attention first. That might suit some briefs, but it changes the planned priority. Ask viewers what they notice before deciding whether it works.

Try it with support

A poster has three unrelated sections with different heading fonts and equal gaps between every item. Suggest changes that could improve unity.

Example reasoning: Repeat a consistent heading treatment for related sections and adjust proximity so each heading is grouped with its own information. Explain which parts belong together. Do not assume every font must be identical if a meaningful hierarchy needs distinction.

Practise and explain

Explain: Can an asymmetrical design be balanced?

Yes. Visual weight depends on factors such as size, tone, position and complexity. Different components can counterbalance one another without being mirror images.

Analyse: Why might six equally dominant headings make a poster harder to use?

They compete for attention and may hide the intended priority. Adjust their relationships so the main message is recognised before supporting information.

Distinguish: How do repetition and proximity support unity in different ways?

Repetition connects components through recurring visual treatment. Proximity connects them by grouping related items spatially. Both can help the viewer recognise relationships.

Redesign the information order

  1. Sketch two versions of the repair café poster using the same content.
  2. Create a different dominant component in each version.
  3. Use proximity and repetition to group related details.
  4. Draw the intended viewing path and annotate the elements supporting it.
  5. Ask a partner what they noticed first; compare the observation with your intention.
  6. Revise one decision and justify it using the brief and feedback.

Evidence to keep: Two compositions, annotated viewing paths, partner observations and one justified revision.

Example response: A defensible version makes the event name dominant, groups date and venue together and directs attention toward the next action. Another order may be justified for a different brief. Use observed behaviour to evaluate the choice rather than treating one layout as universally correct.

A fresh context: a festival guide

A festival page gives its title, sponsor list and “Find a stage” control equal prominence. Visitors mainly need to locate their next event.

Choose a dominant item, group related information and describe the viewing path you would test.

Compare with an example response

Make the stage-finding control prominent and group event names with locations. A possible path is guide title → stage finder → event details. Test whether visitors can find their next stage; do not assume the largest title always serves the task.

Exit question

Write a three-part annotation: visual choice → design principle → audience need.

4. Typography, grids and composition

Recall

  • Explain how size and tone can establish emphasis.
  • Recall how proximity can connect a heading with its supporting information.

Visual walkthrough

Use a grid to organise; use thirds to explore

Pause and predict: The date and venue almost line up, but their left edges differ slightly. What would a shared grid line help you change?

Show the explanation

Align their left edges deliberately. Consistent alignment can make their relationship easier to follow and removes an accidental near-alignment.

Try a small change: Your design looks clear on a large editing screen but the venue is hard to read on a phone. What should you revise and retest?

Compare your reasoning

Try a larger venue label, clearer spacing or stronger tonal separation. Inspect the actual phone-sized output and ask someone to locate the venue again.

Try it with support

A mobile poster uses centred paragraphs, tight line spacing and a very small venue label. Choose two changes and explain how you would check them.

Example reasoning: For example, use a consistent left alignment for multi-line practical information and increase the separation or prominence of the venue label. Inspect the actual output and ask a viewer to find the venue. Record whether the changes address the observed difficulty.

Practise and explain

Distinguish: How is typeface different from size?

Typeface describes the letter design family; size describes how large the letters are displayed or printed. Both influence the appearance and readability of text.

Apply: How could a grid help when the date, image and heading appear disconnected?

Choose common alignment lines and consistent margins, then position related components against them. Check that the resulting groupings express the information relationships.

Evaluate: A partner finds the date quickly but misses the booking instruction. What should your evaluation include?

Identify the successful date treatment and the unsuccessful instruction, connect both observations to criteria, propose a specific revision and describe how you would retest it.

Finish and evaluate a composition

  1. Create two layouts using the same brief, with one based on a grid and one exploring the rule of thirds.
  2. Specify typeface, size, alignment, formatting and spacing decisions.
  3. Select the better-supported layout and add five detailed annotations.
  4. Ask a viewer to find the date, venue and next action at the intended output size.
  5. Record observations, revise one issue and compare the result with the brief.

Evidence to keep: Two layouts, five annotations, a task-based feedback record and a revised design.

Example response: There is no single prescribed layout. A strong response shows deliberate alternatives, consistent alignment, readable practical information and a revision supported by feedback. Explain why the selected arrangement serves this brief better than the alternative.

A fresh context: a canteen menu

A phone menu uses the same text size for food names, prices and section headings. Prices almost line up.

Sketch a clearer hierarchy and alignment. Explain how you would test it with a student.

Compare with an example response

Use distinct section headings, consistent item text and a shared alignment for prices. Keep each price clearly associated with its item. Ask a student to find an item and its price on the phone-sized output, then revise any confusion.

Exit question

Choose one annotation from your design. Improve it by adding a precise effect and a way to test it.
